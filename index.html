About Me

While I've always had a strong inclination for singing, I never paid attention to my technique or approach to it until I began taking vocal lessons. The first couple times, I remember questioning my vocal teachers' methods, not realizing the significance of the whiny meows, singing through a straw or squatting down when attempting to belt a higher note. However, I followed along and took her notes seriously, even when it required re-learning how to breathe. Around 6 months into vocal lessons, my vocal teacher signed me up for a Masterclass with Sharon Szymanski, a former UNC-CH voice teacher. Through listening to her critique of other singers and comments on my own performance, I realized there was more to vocal exercises than just “warming up the voice”. I began to notice the significant difference in my singing abilities before and after making use of these techniques, and for once I was able to grasp why. From Sharon’s masterclass, we broke down the different parts of the exercise, and which parts of the body it was attempting to connect. For example, I learned that blowing through a straw on different pitches creates what is known as “a semi-occluded vocal tract”, which helps the singer to balance air pressure above and below the vocal cords, reducing vocal strain. Simultaneously, this exercise focuses on strengthening the diaphragm to create more space for air when singing, as well as allowing the brain to replicate the same feeling when singing without the straw. Through learning the intent behind these vocal exercises, I fostered a deep interest in the neurological and physiological mechanisms that improve vocal agility and strength over time. By repeatedly questioning the rationale behind each warmup and brain exercise my teacher prompted me to use, I was able to build a comprehensive list of exercises along with the category they fit into, whether it be SOVT exercises, breath control exercises or tricks to help the brain adjust to new skills such as belting or singing in the mixed voice. With her guidance, I began teaching voice lessons to younger kids, testing different exercises in accordance to what they needed assistance with the most. Through learning about the physical processes of what was happening behind every activity, I was able to cater to the needs of the students, as well as my own when practicing music. In this process, I’ve found that questioning the intent behind what has been taught has been incredibly useful in strengthening my skills and confidence in an area. At UNC Chapel Hill, I hope to get involved with research regarding Music and its therapeutic uses. I also plan to join more healthcare-related clubs including the Red Cross Chapter here, and earn my EMT certification in the summer.
