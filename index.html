About Me

While introducing the class to new concepts in elementary school, my teachers repeatedly used this expression until it was engraved in my brain: “Before you can run, you must first learn how to walk”. Through taking vocal lessons over the past 10 years, I have come to a subsequent realization: “Before you can sing, you must first learn how to breathe”.

I remember questioning the various exercises that my instructor taught me, unable to understand why she would have me lie down flat on the floor and practice taking deep breaths, adamant that I keep my shoulders from rising when preparing to sing a note. I didn’t understand the meaning of “proper air support”, and how the approach to taking in a breath could make or break my ability to stay in the center of the pitch. It never occurred to me that something as fundamental to humans as breathing could be performed “incorrectly”. Furthermore, I never felt compelled to look deeper into the consequences of improper breathing or where it stems from. Despite this, I followed along and took her notes into serious consideration, even when it meant reconstructing my perception of something I believed to be completely intuitive.

As I continued masterclasses with vocal experts, they explained the physiology behind “breath support” in singing. In one such session, we put great focus on the use of the straw in reducing vocal strain through the formation of a “semi-occluded” vocal tract, which helps the singer to balance air pressure above and below the vocal cords. Through playing around with the exercise, I learned that it also focuses on strengthening the diaphragm to create more space for air when singing, as well as allowing the brain to replicate the same feeling when singing without the straw. Through learning the intent behind these vocal exercises, I fostered a deep interest in the physiological mechanisms, specifically involving “correct” techniques of breathing that improve vocal agility and strength over time.

About a year later, I had the opportunity to shadow a physician at the Cleveland Clinic, in Ohio. With a deeper interest to understand the physiology behind breathing, I chose to work with a pulmonologist. At our first meeting, I asked him about a book I had read earlier in the year, Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art, discussing how changes in lifestyle such as diet, have considerably affected the way in which humans breathe. He agreed, affirming that there is in fact a large-scale issue in how people breathe; Many have shifted from nasal breathing to breathing out the mouth, consequently exacerbating pulmonology-related health disorders such as sleep apnea and snoring. Following this, I shadowed a pulmonary therapist within the same department who informed me of specific breathing exercises meant for individuals with respiratory issues including COPD and asthma. I was intrigued by the similar engagement of the diaphragm in the exercises she demonstrated to me, and the vocal exercises I was familiar with. Through this experience I understood that breathing was not just something that had to be revised for singing, but something that was actively being reintroduced to patients to improve their quality of life.

Using what I had learned, I built a comprehensive list of different breath control exercises and began implementing them into the voice lessons I was teaching to younger kids. I noticed the effects of deep-breathing solidify over time, as their posture became more upright as they sang and the air expanded out through their ribs, rather than up through their shoulders. As I taught and corrected their technique, I found myself constantly drawing parallels to the exercises I learned about from the pulmonary therapist. Being able to directly apply my knowledge about breath control and observe the changes over time is what sparked my desire to become a physician.

Taking vocal lessons over the past ten years has made me hyperaware of the inner workings of the human body, and has prompted me to explore its extension into various aspects of my life. In this process, I’ve found that questioning the intent behind what has been taught has been incredibly useful in strengthening my skills and confidence for something I am passionate about. At UNC Chapel Hill, I’m a member of the Cadence, an all-girls a-capella group in which the importance of correct breathing continues to be emphasized throughout each rehearsal. In college, I’m eager to learn more about the therapeutic uses of music and the impact it has on the brain, as I continue on my track to major in Neuroscience.
