Neha Zacharia

Research Statement

I am a passionate researcher with a strong interest in computational fluid dynamics and its applications in medical research. My work focuses on understanding the hemodynamic changes in patients who have undergone the Fontan procedure, a complex surgical intervention for congenital heart disease. I aim to contribute to the development of better treatment strategies and improve outcomes for these patients.

Research Experience

In my last research opportunity, I worked on multiple projects with a professor specialized in computational fluid dynamics at the Boston Children’s Hospital. In the first project, the objective was to analyze the evolving hemodynamics in patients with an interrupted inferior vena cava who had undergone the Fontan procedure. Under the supervision of this professor, I worked with a team of engineers and medical students to segment the superior vena cava, azygos veins, pulmonary arteries, and hepatic vessels, including the Hepatozygous shunt connecting them to the azygous vein from 17 different patient DICOM files. Through this process I was able to build a 3D model of the patient-specific anatomy on the Slicer segmentation platform. After this step, I would import the model into the Fusion 360 platform, to smooth out the geometries and trim and extend the inlets and outlets of the model. After the model was smoothed and modified, computational fluid dynamics was used to analyze changes in hemodynamics. This study specially focused on the impact of age on blood flow dominance in individuals who had undergone the fontan procedure. The results showed that as age increased, the lower body was contributing more blood to the heart than the upper body. The results demonstrate the significance of this project, raising questions on what measures should be taken next to either prevent the changes in blood flow dominance or be able to balance it out after the operation.

The second project used the same segmentation and computational fluid dynamic techniques to compare the changes in hemodynamics in Fontan patients to Bilateral Bidirectional Glenn patients. The Glenn procedure in individuals with two superior vena cavae is unique, because it connects the superior vena cava to bidirectional pulmonary arteries. This can lead to flow collisions, which was the main focus of this study. I had to learn to segment a different cardiovascular anatomy featuring the same parts from the first study including the Superior vena cavae, the bronchocephalic veins and pulmonary arteries as well as the hepatic veins.
